Enrichment for animals

About these guides: Information on this page is compiled and summarised from SAIS material and external sources. Where external guidance is used, we aim to link to the original source so you can check its full context and latest version. See our Sources & editorial standards. This is general information, not veterinary advice.

Practical ideas to encourage natural behaviours, curiosity, movement, problem-solving and choice. Enrichment should suit the individual animal and always put welfare first.

Explore enrichment ideas ↓
Choice · Curiosity · Wellbeing
Safety comes first

Supervise new activities, check toys and materials for damage, and remove anything that could be swallowed, cause entanglement or injure an animal. Introduce activities gradually and stop if an animal appears worried or frustrated. Enrichment is not a substitute for appropriate housing, companionship, exercise or veterinary care.

Dogs

Enrichment for dogs

Give dogs safe opportunities to sniff, forage, chew, explore and make choices.

01 · Food and problem-solving

Food enrichment and puzzle toys

Food-dispensing toys and puzzle feeders can turn part of a meal into an activity. Start with an easy setup and increase difficulty only if your dog remains comfortable. Choose a size and material suitable for your dog, supervise use, and check toys for damage. Account for treats in the daily food allowance.

One example is K9 Connectables, modular food-enrichment toys that link together in different configurations. Always follow the manufacturer's safety instructions.

02 · Sniffing and choice

Sniffari walks

A sniffari is a relaxed walk where a dog is given time to investigate scents and choose where to sniff, within safe boundaries. Use a secure lead or long line in an appropriate open area, away from roads and hazards. Keep your dog under control, respect wildlife and livestock, and do not use a long line where it could create a risk.

Sniffing provides mental engagement and lets dogs explore information through scent. The walk need not be long: follow your dog's comfort and fitness, offer breaks, and praise voluntary check-ins.

03 · Home environment

Garden adventures

A garden can offer a small sensory exploration area without needing a complete redesign. Consider safe pathways, different stable surfaces, tunnels, a digging area, sturdy platforms at ground level, or a shallow paddling pool if your dog enjoys it and can enter and leave safely.

Only use plants confirmed safe for dogs, avoid pesticides and fertilisers where animals may contact them, and provide shade, water and a quiet place to rest. Do not assume a plant is safe because it is commonly used in gardens.

04 · Play and chewing

Play that suits your dog

Try short games of fetch, scent searches, simple training, tug with clear start-and-stop cues, or safe chew items. Adapt games to age, health, mobility and temperament. Avoid repetitive high-impact exercise for dogs who may be vulnerable to injury, and ask your vet if you are unsure what is suitable.

Cats

Enrichment for cats

Support hunting, climbing, scratching, hiding and exploring.

Interactive play

Use feather wands or other cat-safe toys to encourage stalking, chasing and pouncing. Keep string and wand toys put away when not supervised. Let the cat catch the toy sometimes, and finish with a calm activity or meal if helpful.

Climbing and scratching

Provide stable cat trees, shelves, window perches and scratching surfaces where possible. Ensure climbing routes are secure and offer lower options for older cats or cats with mobility problems.

Foraging and puzzle feeders

Scatter a small portion of the daily food allowance or use suitable puzzle feeders. Start simple and make sure the cat can access enough food without frustration.

Hiding and exploring

Cardboard boxes, tunnels, covered beds and safe places to observe from can help cats feel secure. Cat grass or catnip may interest some cats; use products intended for cats and stop if they cause unwanted reactions.

Rabbits and guinea pigs

Enrichment for rabbits and guinea pigs

Encourage foraging, chewing, hiding and movement while preserving safe space and companionship.

Rabbits

Offer plenty of suitable hay, safe tunnels, hideouts with more than one exit where practical, and opportunities to explore and dig in a supervised, secure area. Untreated cardboard and rabbit-safe willow items may be useful if they are not treated with inks, glue or chemicals that could be harmful. Rabbits need ample space and compatible rabbit companionship; toys do not replace these needs.

Guinea pigs

Provide multiple hideaways, tunnels, safe areas to move, and opportunities to forage for hay and suitable leafy greens. Use safe, untreated chew materials and check that tunnels and hideouts are appropriately sized. Keep food changes gradual and follow species-appropriate dietary guidance.

Small mammals

Enrichment for gerbils, hamsters, rats, degus and chinchillas

Small mammals have different needs, so choose equipment and substrates for the specific species.

Gerbils

Provide a deep area of suitable burrowing substrate, nesting materials, safe cardboard to shred, and sturdy objects to explore. Use a solid-surface exercise wheel of an appropriate size and avoid wire or mesh running surfaces.

Hamsters

Offer deep suitable bedding for burrowing, a secure hide, safe chew materials, and a correctly sized solid-surface wheel. A suitable sand bath may be beneficial for some species; use appropriate sand rather than dusty products. Check that all items fit the hamster and enclosure safely.

Rats

Rats often benefit from climbing opportunities, hammocks, ropes, tunnels, nesting material and food-foraging games. Provide safe out-of-enclosure exploration only in a secure, supervised area, and ensure rats have appropriate social companionship.

Degus

Offer safe chewing materials, platforms and hides, opportunities to forage, and a suitable solid running wheel. Degus have specific dietary and social needs; avoid sugary treats and seek species-specific advice when choosing food or equipment.

Chinchillas

Provide secure shelves and ledges for jumping, safe chew items, hides and appropriate dust-bathing opportunities. Chinchillas are agile and can be injured by unsuitable falls or poorly designed equipment, so check spacing and stability carefully.

Birds and other exotics

Enrichment for birds, reptiles and other exotics

Environmental needs vary greatly between species. Research the exact species before changing an enclosure.

Birds

Depending on species, enrichment may include varied safe perches, shreddable and chewable bird-safe materials, foraging activities, bathing opportunities and positive interaction. Avoid unsafe strings, toxic materials and toys that can trap toes or necks. Provide suitable flight and movement opportunities for the species.

Reptiles and amphibians

Use appropriate hides, climbing structures, substrate, visual barriers and opportunities to explore. Correct temperature gradients, lighting, humidity and access to water are essential parts of welfare, not optional extras. Only use plants and materials suitable for the species and enclosure conditions.

Ferrets

Offer supervised exploration, tunnels, sturdy play objects, digging opportunities and food-based games suited to the ferret. Secure gaps, cables, small swallowable objects and household hazards before out-of-enclosure play.

Sugar gliders and other specialist pets

These animals have specialised housing, diet, social and activity needs. Seek advice from a vet experienced with the species or a reputable specialist welfare organisation before introducing toys, climbing equipment or feeding activities.
