Health & safety for pets

Practical ways to reduce everyday risks, recognise when an animal may be distressed, and know where to turn for reliable help.

Explore safety guidance ↓
Prevention · Awareness · Welfare
Important: general information, not a diagnosis.

If an animal struggles to breathe, collapses, has severe bleeding, may have swallowed a toxin, is in significant pain or rapidly deteriorates, contact a veterinary practice or emergency veterinary service immediately. Do not wait for an online reply.

Everyday prevention

General animal safety

Open a topic for practical reminders. Adapt advice to the species and individual animal.

1. Boundaries and body language+
  • Give unfamiliar animals space and allow them to approach voluntarily.
  • Learn species-specific signals. A wagging dog's tail does not always mean friendliness; cats may flatten their ears or lash their tail, and horses may pin their ears or tense up.
  • If an animal freezes, retreats, growls, hisses or appears uncomfortable, stop interacting and give it space. Never punish warning signals.
  • Avoid disturbing animals while they eat, sleep, care for young or guard a valued item. Closely supervise children around all animals.
2. Safe approach and handling+
  • Move calmly, avoid looming over an unfamiliar animal and leave it a clear route to move away.
  • Do not corner, chase, hug, restrain or pick up an animal unless necessary for welfare and safe to do so.
  • Use species-appropriate handling. Gloves alone do not make handling a frightened, injured or unfamiliar animal safe.
  • For livestock, horses, reptiles and birds, seek guidance from a competent handler. Keep your distance from an animal that is injured or behaving unusually.
3. Health checks and warning signs+

Know the animal's normal appetite, drinking, breathing, movement, toileting and behaviour. Changes may be early signs of pain or illness.

  • Seek urgent veterinary advice for breathing difficulty, collapse, seizures, severe bleeding, suspected poisoning, heatstroke, major trauma or sudden deterioration.
  • Contact a vet promptly for not eating, persistent vomiting or diarrhoea, limping, wounds, unusual discharge or behaviour changes. Rabbits, birds and small animals can deteriorate quickly.
  • Never give human medicines or leftover veterinary medicines unless a vet specifically directs you to.
  • Vaccination and preventative care depend on species, age, lifestyle and location. Ask your vet what is appropriate.
4. Home, garden and poisoning risks+
  • Secure medicines, cleaning products, pesticides, slug pellets, antifreeze and other chemicals in their original containers.
  • Check plants and foods for species-specific toxicity. Lilies are particularly dangerous to cats.
  • Keep batteries, string, sharp items, small objects and unsuitable foods out of reach.
  • Provide suitable housing, ventilation, shade, clean water and safe temperatures; protect animals from escape, predators and weather extremes.
  • Never leave an animal unattended in a vehicle where it could overheat or become distressed.

If poisoning is possible, contact a vet immediately and keep packaging or plant details available. Do not induce vomiting or try a home remedy unless a veterinary professional instructs you to.

5. Hygiene and infection prevention+
  • Wash hands with soap and water after handling animals, feed, bedding, faeces, reptile equipment or enclosures, and before preparing food.
  • Keep animal food and equipment separate from human food preparation areas. Clean items using products suitable for the species.
  • Cover cuts before animal care. Avoid kissing animals or letting them lick your face, wounds or medical devices.
  • Some people may need extra precautions around certain animals or infections; seek healthcare advice if concerned.
6. Children, visitors and allergies+
  • An adult should actively supervise children around animals. Never leave a baby or young child alone with a pet.
  • Teach children to let animals approach, avoid disturbing them while eating or resting, and ask before touching unfamiliar animals.
  • Check for allergies or sensitivities before bringing animals into shared spaces and follow any individual allergy action plan.
7. Wildlife and unfamiliar animals+
  • Observe wildlife from a distance. Do not handle injured wildlife with bare hands; contact a reputable wildlife rescue or the RSPCA for advice.
  • Keep pets and children away while help is arranged. Do not feed unsuitable foods or interfere with nests and young animals.
  • Do not approach livestock or horses without understanding the risks. Keep dogs under close control and follow signs and access guidance.
8. Walking dogs near roads+
  • Use a secure, well-fitted collar or harness and a lead you can control. Keep dogs on a lead near roads and whenever needed to protect people, animals or wildlife.
  • Use pavements where available. If there is no pavement, follow the Highway Code for pedestrians and keep the dog away from traffic where safe and practical.
  • Wear reflective clothing or use lights in poor visibility. Stay alert and avoid phone use or headphones that distract you near traffic.
  • Training can help, but never rely on a cue alone to protect a dog from traffic. Carry water in warm weather and adjust walks to the dog's age, health and fitness.
  • Check paws after walks and avoid sharp litter, contaminated water, road salt and chemical spills.

Hot surfaces: no simple hand test guarantees pavement safety for every dog. Consider temperature, sun, surface and duration; choose cooler times and stop if your dog shows discomfort.

9. Identification, restraint and the law+
  • In England, Scotland and Wales, dogs in public generally must wear a collar with the owner's name and address, subject to exceptions. Dogs must also be microchipped under applicable rules; keep database details up to date.
  • In vehicles, dogs must be suitably restrained so they cannot distract the driver or injure themselves or others. Check the current Highway Code and equipment instructions.
  • Keep dogs under control around livestock and follow signs, local rules and current legislation. Requirements may differ across the UK.
10. Body cameras and incident recording+

A body camera is a personal choice, not a general UK safety requirement for dog owners. It does not replace awareness, safe handling or keeping distance from conflict.

If recording, consider privacy and data-protection responsibilities, especially when identifiable people or private property are captured or footage is shared online. In an incident, prioritise safety and seek help first.

11. Animal bites and scratches+
  • Move away safely without trying to grab or punish the animal.
  • If a bite or scratch breaks the skin, wash it thoroughly with soap and running water and seek prompt medical advice, especially for deep wounds, hand or face injuries, infection signs or possible exposure abroad.
  • Use NHS 111 for urgent advice where appropriate; call 999 for a life-threatening emergency or serious uncontrolled bleeding.
  • Report dangerous incidents to the appropriate local authority or police as relevant.
12. Prepare for an emergency+
  • Save your vet's daytime and out-of-hours contact details where they can be found quickly.
  • Keep suitable transport equipment ready, plus prescribed medicines and relevant medical information.
  • Plan how to move animals safely during a household emergency and ensure anyone caring for them knows the arrangements.
  • Keep identification and microchip registration details up to date.
