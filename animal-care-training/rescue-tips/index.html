Rescue and sanctuary guidance

Rescue Tips

Questions to consider when choosing a rescue or sanctuary to support. These tips have been shared with SAIS by a registered charity rescue organisation.

What to look for

About this guidance

The information on this page was supplied by a registered rescue organisation. It is intended as a general checklist, not a formal inspection or a guarantee that an organisation is suitable. Registration status and practices should be checked independently.

Rescues and sanctuaries

What to look for

Personal recommendations can be a useful starting point when choosing a rescue or sanctuary to support. However, independent research and due diligence are always advisable. Use the points below as a guide, and ask reasonable questions before donating, volunteering or adopting.

1. Transparency

  • Clear purpose and governance: the organisation explains its mission, objectives and how it operates, and can provide evidence of relevant registration or compliance where applicable.
  • Financial accountability: it explains how donations are used and provides appropriate financial information. You can ask how your contribution will be spent; the level of detail available may vary by organisation and legal status.
  • Animal updates: it shares regular, credible updates about the animals in its care, ideally including authentic photos or videos.
  • Day-to-day welfare: it can explain feeding, cleaning, enrichment, veterinary care and how animals are given suitable opportunities to display natural behaviours.
  • Policies and contact details: policies and procedures are clear, contact information is visible, and staff are willing to answer reasonable questions.

2. Professionalism

  • Staff and volunteers communicate respectfully and provide accurate information about the animals and their needs.
  • Where rehoming is offered, there is a suitable assessment process. This may include an application, home check and veterinary references depending on the animal and circumstances.
  • The organisation can discuss its rehoming experience honestly, including how it learns from placements that do not work out.
  • Animal welfare is prioritised, including appropriate food, suitable accommodation, enrichment, preventive care and veterinary treatment when needed.
  • The organisation is willing to work with other welfare organisations and relevant professionals where appropriate.
  • Where applicable, it provides clear rescue back-up (RBU), including post-adoption advice and a plan if a placement breaks down.

3. Potential red flags

  • Evasive answers, unclear contact details, or information that is inconsistent, limited or difficult to verify.
  • Unprofessional or hostile responses to reasonable questions, including on social media.
  • Little meaningful evidence of animals’ living conditions, welfare routines or veterinary care.
  • No clear adoption assessment or no explanation of what happens if an adoption does not work out.
  • Repeated unsuccessful placements without transparency about what has been learned or changed.
  • Frequent urgent fundraising appeals with little explanation of how funds are used, or unwillingness to provide reasonable supporting information.
  • Visible signs that animals may be neglected, unwell or lacking suitable care.
Use judgement, not a single test

One concern does not automatically prove wrongdoing, and small organisations may have limited resources. Look at the overall evidence, ask follow-up questions and contact the relevant regulator or charity commission if you have a specific concern.

Useful checks

  • GOV.UK — Search the charity register (England and Wales)
  • Charity Commission for England and Wales

Not every legitimate rescue or sanctuary is a registered charity. Check the organisation’s legal status and the appropriate regulator for its location and structure.
