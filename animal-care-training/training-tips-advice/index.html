Training Tips & Advice

About these guides: Information on this page is compiled and summarised from SAIS material and external sources. Where external guidance is used, we aim to link to the original source so you can check its full context and latest version. See our Sources & editorial standards. This is general information, not veterinary advice.

Practical, positive training ideas for everyday life with your dog — from responding to their name to travelling calmly in the car. These tips were supplied by Small and Mighty Pet Training in Taunton, UK.

Before you start

Keep sessions short, calm and rewarding. Let your dog work at their own pace and make the exercise easier if they seem confused, worried or frustrated. Use a reward your dog values, and stop before they become tired. These are general training tips, not a substitute for veterinary or behaviour support.

Contributor: Small and Mighty Pet Training, Taunton, UK. The original material supplied for this page credits their team.

Step-by-step guides

Everyday training skills

Choose a guide below. You do not need to teach everything at once — practise one skill at a time.

01 · Attention

The name game

Helping your dog learn that their name is a cue to check in with you can support communication and later recall training. Their name is not, by itself, a guarantee of recall.

  1. Set aside some of your dog's normal daily food or prepare small, suitable treats.
  2. In a quiet place, say your dog's name once in a friendly voice.
  3. If they turn towards you or look at you, immediately mark the response with a cheerful “yes” and give a few small pieces of food, or one reward.
  4. Pause and let your dog look away naturally. Say their name again when they are ready.
  5. Repeat for a few successful turns, then finish. Keep the session easy and enjoyable.
Timing tip

Reward as soon as your dog turns towards you. If they do not respond, avoid repeating their name rapidly; reduce distractions, give them a moment, or try again later.

Practice plan: Try a few short sessions throughout the day, provided your dog is comfortable and interested. There is no need to force a fixed number of repetitions or train when they are tired.

02 · Everyday cue

Teaching “sit”

A sit can be useful in everyday routines, but it should be taught as a comfortable, voluntary behaviour. Some dogs may find sitting uncomfortable, particularly if they have pain or mobility problems.

  1. Choose a quiet area and have small, appealing treats ready.
  2. Stand or kneel in front of your dog without crowding them.
  3. Hold a treat near their nose and slowly move it slightly upwards and back, allowing their head to follow it.
  4. As their bottom naturally reaches the floor, say “sit” once and reward immediately.
  5. Pause between repetitions. Keep each session brief and stop while your dog is still engaged.
  6. As they understand the movement, gradually reduce the lure and reward the response. Continue to reward regularly, especially in new places.
  7. Practise in different calm settings before adding distractions. Increase difficulty gradually rather than expecting a reliable response everywhere straight away.
Keep it positive

Do not push your dog's bottom down. If they jump up to follow the treat, move more slowly or reward a smaller step. Praise and food can both be useful rewards.

03 · Trick training

Teaching “paw”

This trick is optional. Only practise if your dog is comfortable having their paws and legs near your hands. Never grab or lift a paw that they are reluctant to offer.

  1. Invite your dog into a sit if that is comfortable for them.
  2. Hold a treat in a closed hand.
  3. Place your closed hand near the side of your dog's cheek, rather than directly pushing into their face. The best position can vary between dogs.
  4. Wait patiently. If your dog lifts a paw towards your hand, mark the moment with “yes” or a click, then reward with the other hand.
  5. Repeat a few times, then take a break. If your dog becomes frustrated, make it easier or stop.
  6. Once your dog reliably offers a paw, say your chosen cue — such as “paw” — just before presenting your hand. Practise the other paw separately if you want to teach both.
What does “mark” mean?

A marker is a consistent sound, such as “yes”, or a clicker sound that tells your dog exactly which behaviour earned the reward. Deliver the reward straight after the marker.

Choose clear cues: For example, use “paw” for one paw and “other” for the other. Keep the cues and your hand signals consistent.

04 · Nose work

Dog tracking and scent work

Dogs use their noses to gather information about the world. Scent activities can provide mental enrichment and a structured way to use this natural ability.

Tracking and trailing

  • Tracking: in a common working definition, the dog follows a trail using ground scent and signs of the route, such as disturbed vegetation.
  • Trailing or mantrailing: the dog follows the scent of a particular person, which can drift away from the exact route they walked.

The terms can be used differently by different sports and organisations. Related activities include recreational scent work, search-and-rescue work, and formal tracking trials. Police and specialist search work require specialist training; they are not the same as a casual pet activity.

How to get started safely

  • Start with an easy, short scent game in a quiet, safe area.
  • Use a comfortable harness and a suitable lead where needed, and keep your dog away from roads, livestock and wildlife.
  • Let the dog investigate without rushing them. Keep the task achievable and end on a positive note.
  • For organised tracking or mantrailing, find a reputable instructor who prioritises welfare and works at each dog's pace.

Dogs of many ages can enjoy suitable scent games, but adapt the activity to their health, confidence and physical ability.

05 · Handling skills

Making collar handling positive

Teaching a dog that a hand reaching towards their collar predicts something good can make everyday handling safer. Build this gradually and never use the exercise to trap or punish your dog.

  1. Start by offering a treat while your dog is relaxed and you briefly touch an area near their neck, only if they are comfortable with this.
  2. Gradually pair a gentle touch to the collar with a treat, then remove your hand.
  3. When your dog remains relaxed, briefly hold the collar and immediately reward, then let go.
  4. Build duration in tiny steps only while your dog stays comfortable. Mix in easy repetitions and finish early.
  5. Practise in different calm settings after the skill is comfortable at home.
Watch your dog's response

If they turn away, freeze, duck, lick their lips repeatedly, growl or try to leave, stop and give them space. Do not punish a growl. If handling is difficult or there is a bite risk, seek qualified behaviour support.

06 · Travel confidence

Helping your puppy feel comfortable in the car

Some puppies need time to become comfortable around a vehicle. Work in small stages and progress only when your puppy is relaxed. If your dog drools, vomits, pants heavily or appears distressed, travel sickness or anxiety may be involved; speak to your vet.

Stage 1: Make the parked car positive

  1. Park somewhere safe, quiet and ventilated. Let your puppy investigate from a comfortable distance.
  2. Use a familiar blanket and small treats if your puppy enjoys food. Never force them into the car.
  3. Reward looking at the car, approaching it and then exploring the doorway. Let them move away whenever they need to.
  4. If they choose to enter, give a treat and allow them to come back out. Repeat briefly only while they remain relaxed.
  5. You can offer a meal in the parked car if your puppy is comfortable eating there. Do not withhold meals to make them enter.

Stage 2: Build calm time inside

  1. When your puppy is happy getting in and out, invite them to settle in the stationary car for a few seconds.
  2. Reward calm moments, gradually adding a little time. If they become worried, shorten the session or return to an easier step.
  3. Introduce their travel restraint gradually and positively, following the manufacturer's fitting instructions.
  4. Only once your puppy is relaxed in the stationary car should you consider briefly turning on the engine, then switch it off and reward calm behaviour.

Stage 3: Very short journeys

  1. Secure your puppy correctly before the car moves. Use a suitable, properly fitted crash-tested harness or secured travel crate; a dog guard may be appropriate for some vehicle setups but does not replace every restraint requirement.
  2. Begin with a very short journey to a quiet destination, if your puppy is comfortable.
  3. Keep journeys gradual and positive, increasing distance only when they cope well. Never reward or handle an unrestrained dog while driving.
Travel safely

Dogs must be suitably restrained so they cannot distract the driver or injure themselves or others. Check current UK Highway Code guidance and your vehicle restraint instructions. Do not leave a dog unattended in a hot car.

If your puppy remains frightened or unwell, pause the training and ask your vet or a qualified force-free trainer for help.

07 · Play and safety

Teaching “drop” during tug

The aim is to teach your dog that releasing a toy does not always mean play is over. Keep tug gentle and suitable for your dog's size and health.

  1. Start a short game of tug with a toy your dog enjoys.
  2. When your dog has hold of the toy, stop moving it and keep your hands still. Avoid pulling hard or trying to prise their mouth open.
  3. Wait quietly for your dog to release the toy. At first, you may need to practise with a less exciting toy or reward a small release.
  4. The moment the toy is released, mark with “yes” and say “drop” calmly, then briefly tuck the toy close to your body.
  5. After a short pause, invite your dog to play again or reward them with a treat, depending on what works best for them.
  6. Repeat a few times, then let your dog enjoy a final round of play or finish calmly.
Build trust around possessions

Do not chase your dog, threaten them or forcibly take items from their mouth. If you need an object, offer a safe trade for something more rewarding. If your dog guards objects, stiffens, growls or snaps, stop the exercise and seek help from a qualified, reward-based behaviour professional.
