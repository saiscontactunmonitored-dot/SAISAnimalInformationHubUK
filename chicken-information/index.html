Backyard Chicken Care Guide

About these guides: Information on this page is compiled and summarised from SAIS material and external sources. Where external guidance is used, we aim to link to the original source so you can check its full context and latest version. See our Sources & editorial standards.   • Secure shelter: Provide a dry, well-ventilated, predator-resistant coop with suitable perches and nesting areas.
  • Outdoor space: Give birds safe room to scratch, forage, dust-bathe and move naturally, with shade and weather protection.
  • Cleaning: Keep bedding dry and housing clean. Check fencing, latches, roofs and wire regularly for damage or predator access.
  • Flock safety: Provide enough accessible feeders, drinkers and resting space so birds can avoid competition and bullying.
The care guide

Six key areas of chicken keeping

Open each section for practical advice. Check current official guidance because disease-control rules and registration requirements can change.

Item Requirement Notes
Daily Feed Layer Pellets or Crumble Rich in calcium and main source of nutrition.
Insoluble Grit Coarse sand/small stones Essential for digestion. Keep a bowl available.
Calcium Supplement Crushed Oyster Shell Keeps eggshells strong, especially in heavy layers.
Important: In the UK, feeding kitchen scraps to poultry is prohibited. Use a complete feed appropriate for the birds’ age and purpose, with suitable poultry grit and clean water. If unsure about an ingredient, do not offer it.
  • Daily Check: Ensure birds are active, bright-eyed, and scratching around. Watch for pale combs or limping.
  • Red mites: Check birds and housing regularly, especially cracks, perches and nest boxes. If you suspect an infestation, seek advice on a suitable treatment from a poultry-experienced vet or qualified adviser; use products only according to their label.
  • Worms and parasites: Monitor birds for weight loss, diarrhoea, reduced laying or other changes. Do not use a fixed worming schedule or medication without appropriate advice; ask a poultry-experienced vet about testing and treatment.
  • Cleaning Schedule: Clear droppings and wet bedding weekly to control ammonia gas levels.
  • Understanding Frostbite: In icy weather, blood flow decreases to combs, wattles, and feet to conserve core heat. Single-comb tips and wet wattles are most vulnerable.
  • Moisture and ventilation: Keep bedding dry and provide good ventilation without creating draughts at perch level. Do not seal the coop so tightly that moisture and ammonia build up.
  • Cold-weather protection: Prioritise dry housing, ventilation and monitoring. Ask a poultry-experienced vet for advice if you are concerned about frostbite; do not apply products unless you know they are suitable for poultry.
  • Dry footing: Reduce muddy areas where possible and provide dry, sheltered places to stand and dust-bathe. Keep bedding clean and dry.
  • Symptoms and care: Pale, grey, blue or damaged combs, wattles or feet may indicate frostbite or another problem. Keep the bird calm and seek prompt veterinary advice rather than rubbing or rapidly heating the affected area.
  • Freezing Water: Check drinkers twice daily to clear ice. Floating a small rubber ball in water tubs helps delay freezing.
📖 Useful Further Reading on Frostbite:
  • Extension Poultry: Frostbite in Chickens
  • PoultryDVM: Frostbite Conditions & Care
  • Laying Age: Most breeds start laying around 18–24 weeks old.
  • Egg Collection: Collect eggs at least once daily so they stay clean and don't get accidentally crushed.
  • Winter Slowdown: Laying naturally decreases when daylight hours drop. This gives hens a healthy period to rest and moult.
  • Choosing a breed: Consider temperament, size, laying expectations, available space, local climate and the bird’s welfare needs. Individual birds vary, so no breed is guaranteed to suit every garden.
  • Social Requirements: Never keep a single chicken. Start with a minimum flock of 3.
  • Registration in England and Wales: Keepers of poultry and other captive birds must register with APHA within one month, including birds kept as pets. Rules and registration routes differ in Scotland, so check the relevant official guidance for your nation.
  • Animal Welfare Act 2006: Keepers are legally bound to provide proper housing, water, diet, and protection from pain and suffering.
  • Local Property Covenants: Backyard coops generally don't require council planning permission, but check your property deeds or tenancy agreement for historical restrictions on poultry.
